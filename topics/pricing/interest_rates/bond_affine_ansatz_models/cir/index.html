    Affine Bond Pricing: CIR Model

    The Cox-Ingersoll-Ross model, despite its non-linear square-root diffusion term, remarkably maintains an affine structure for bond pricing 1. This means we can derive closed-form solutions for zero-coupon bonds, a remarkable result given the path-dependent nature of stochastic interest rates.

    Before diving in, make sure you're familiar with the CIR model fundamentals, including the square-root diffusion, Feller condition, and distributional properties. The affine structure represents one of the model's most valuable features, enabling analytical tractability while maintaining the desirable property of non-negative interest rates 2.

    The Bond Pricing Problem

    We seek the price at time \(t\) of a zero-coupon bond maturing at time \(T\), denoted \(P(t, T)\). Under the risk-neutral pricing framework 2, the bond price equals the expected discounted payoff:

    \[ \begin{equation} P(t, T) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\exp\left(-\int_t^T r(s)ds\right) \bigg| \mathcal{F}_t\right], \label{eq:cir_bond_price_expectation} \end{equation} \]

    where:

    • \(\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\) denotes expectation under the risk-neutral measure
    • \(\mathcal{F}_t\) is the information available at time \(t\)
    • \(r(s)\) follows the CIR dynamics: \(dr(s) = a(b - r(s))ds + \sigma\sqrt{r(s)}\,dW(s)\) 1

    The square-root volatility makes this expectation non-trivial, but remarkably the affine ansatz still works 2. This is particularly valuable as it preserves computational tractability while ensuring positive interest rates.

    The Affine Ansatz

    Following the affine term structure methodology 2, we conjecture that the bond price has the exponential-affine form:

    \[ \begin{equation} P(t, T) = A(t, T)e^{-B(t, T)r(t)}, \label{eq:cir_affine_ansatz} \end{equation} \]

    where \(A(t, T) > 0\) and \(B(t, T) \geq 0\) are deterministic functions to be determined.

    The boundary condition at maturity is:

    \[ \begin{equation} P(T, T) = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad A(T, T) = 1, \quad B(T, T) = 0. \label{eq:cir_boundary_conditions} \end{equation} \]

    Deriving the Pricing PDE

    Under the risk-neutral measure, applying the Feynman-Kac theorem 2, the bond price must satisfy:

    \[ \begin{equation} \frac{\partial P}{\partial t} + a(b - r)\frac{\partial P}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 r\frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial r^2} - rP = 0. \label{eq:cir_pricing_pde} \end{equation} \]

    Note the key difference from the Vasicek model 3: the diffusion coefficient is \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 r\) instead of \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\), reflecting the state-dependent volatility that ensures non-negativity 1.

    Computing the partial derivatives of \(P = A(t,T)e^{-B(t,T)r(t)}\):

    \[ \begin{equation} \frac{\partial P}{\partial t} = \left(\frac{\partial A}{A\partial t} - r\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}\right)P, \label{eq:cir_dP_dt} \end{equation} \]
    \[ \begin{equation} \frac{\partial P}{\partial r} = -BP, \label{eq:cir_dP_dr} \end{equation} \]
    \[ \begin{equation} \frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial r^2} = B^2P. \label{eq:cir_d2P_dr2} \end{equation} \]

    Substituting into the PDE

    Plugging these into equation \(\ref{eq:cir_pricing_pde}\):

    \[ \begin{equation} \left(\frac{\partial A}{A\partial t} - r\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}\right)P - a(b - r)BP + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 rB^2P - rP = 0. \label{eq:cir_pde_substituted} \end{equation} \]

    Dividing by \(P\) and rearranging by powers of \(r\):

    \[ \begin{equation} \frac{\partial A}{A\partial t} - abB + r\left(aB + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 B^2 - \frac{\partial B}{\partial t} - 1\right) = 0. \label{eq:cir_pde_rearranged} \end{equation} \]

    For this to hold for all \(r\), both the constant term and the coefficient of \(r\) must vanish.

    Solving for \(B(t, T)\)

    The coefficient of \(r\) gives us:

    \[ \begin{equation} \frac{\partial B}{\partial t} + aB + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 B^2 - 1 = 0, \label{eq:cir_B_ode} \end{equation} \]

    with boundary condition \(B(T, T) = 0\).

    This is a Riccati equation (non-linear ODE), a hallmark of models with state-dependent volatility 1. The closed-form solution, derived in 2, is:

    \[ \begin{equation} B(t, T) = \frac{2(e^{\gamma(T-t)} - 1)}{(\gamma + a)(e^{\gamma(T-t)} - 1) + 2\gamma}, \label{eq:cir_B_solution} \end{equation} \]

    where:

    \[ \begin{equation} \gamma = \sqrt{a^2 + 2\sigma^2}. \label{eq:gamma_definition} \end{equation} \]

    Interpretation:

    • \(\gamma\) represents an adjusted mean reversion speed accounting for volatility, incorporating both the mean reversion parameter \(a\) and the volatility \(\sigma\) 1
    • As \(T - t \to 0\), we have \(B(t, T) \to 0\) (approaching maturity)
    • The non-linear \(B^2\) term in the ODE arises from the square-root volatility structure
    • When \(\sigma = 0\), we have \(\gamma = a\) and recover a simpler exponential form similar to the Vasicek model 2

    Solving for \(A(t, T)\)

    The constant term gives us:

    \[ \begin{equation} \frac{\partial \ln A}{\partial t} - abB(t, T) = 0, \label{eq:cir_A_ode} \end{equation} \]

    with boundary condition \(A(T, T) = 1\).

    Integrating from \(t\) to \(T\):

    \[ \begin{equation} \ln A(t, T) = ab\int_t^T B(u, T)du. \label{eq:cir_ln_A_integral} \end{equation} \]

    After computing this integral using the solution for \(B(t,T)\), we get:

    \[ \begin{equation} A(t, T) = \left[\frac{2\gamma e^{(a+\gamma)(T-t)/2}}{(\gamma + a)(e^{\gamma(T-t)} - 1) + 2\gamma}\right]^{2ab/\sigma^2}. \label{eq:cir_A_solution} \end{equation} \]

    Interpretation:

    • The exponent \(\frac{2ab}{\sigma^2}\) is the degrees of freedom in the chi-squared distribution, directly related to the Feller condition 1
    • \(A(t,T)\) captures the convexity adjustment and average effect of future rates
    • Unlike the Vasicek model 3, \(A(t,T)\) has a more complex form due to the non-linear volatility structure 2

    Complete Bond Pricing Formula

    Putting everything together, the zero-coupon bond price under the CIR model is:

    \[ \begin{equation} P(t, T) = A(t, T)\exp\left(-B(t, T)r(t)\right), \label{eq:cir_bond_complete} \end{equation} \]

    where:

    \[ \begin{equation} \gamma = \sqrt{a^2 + 2\sigma^2}, \label{eq:gamma_final} \end{equation} \]
    \[ \begin{equation} B(t, T) = \frac{2(e^{\gamma(T-t)} - 1)}{(\gamma + a)(e^{\gamma(T-t)} - 1) + 2\gamma}, \label{eq:cir_B_final} \end{equation} \]
    \[ \begin{equation} A(t, T) = \left[\frac{2\gamma e^{(a+\gamma)(T-t)/2}}{(\gamma + a)(e^{\gamma(T-t)} - 1) + 2\gamma}\right]^{2ab/\sigma^2}. \label{eq:cir_A_final} \end{equation} \]

    This is a closed-form solution despite the non-linear square-root diffusion 1! The analytical tractability makes the CIR model particularly valuable for practical applications in fixed income pricing and risk management 2.

    Yield Curve Implications

    The continuously compounded yield is:

    \[ \begin{equation} y(t, T) = -\frac{\ln P(t, T)}{T - t} = -\frac{\ln A(t, T)}{T - t} + \frac{B(t, T)}{T - t}r(t). \label{eq:cir_yield} \end{equation} \]

    Like the Vasicek model 3, yields are affine in the current short rate, but the functions \(A\) and \(B\) have different forms due to the state-dependent volatility 2.

    Key differences from Vasicek 3:

    • The rate-sensitivity curve \(B(t,T)\) has a different shape due to the Riccati equation arising from state-dependent volatility 1
    • Higher volatility \(\sigma\) affects both the shape and level of the yield curve through \(\gamma\)
    • The model naturally incorporates volatility-dependent convexity adjustments, providing more realistic term structure dynamics 2

    Comparison with Vasicek

    Feature Vasicek CIR
    Diffusion Constant \(\sigma\) Square-root \(\sigma\sqrt{r(t)}\)
    Negative rates Possible Prevented (with Feller condition)
    \(B(t,T)\) ODE Linear Riccati (non-linear)
    \(B(t,T)\) form Simple exponential More complex
    \(A(t,T)\) form Exponential with quadratic terms Power function
    Calibration Easier (linear) More complex (non-linear)

    Both models maintain the affine structure, making them computationally tractable despite their differences 2. The CIR model's additional complexity is justified by its prevention of negative interest rates, a critical feature in many applications 1.

    Practical Applications

    The CIR bond pricing formula enables various practical applications in fixed income markets 2:

    1. Calibration: Fit \((a, b, \sigma)\) to observed bond prices, ensuring the Feller condition \(2ab \geq \sigma^2\) 1
    2. Options pricing: Use bond prices in formulas for caps, floors, and swaptions 4
    3. Risk management: Compute duration and convexity analytically, accounting for rate-dependent volatility
    4. Scenario analysis: Model rate paths that remain non-negative, particularly important in low-rate environments
    5. Credit modeling: Use as intensity process for default risk, exploiting the non-negativity constraint 2

    The non-negativity property makes CIR particularly valuable when modeling credit spreads, default intensities, or in low-rate environments where negative rates would be economically problematic 1.

    Extensions

    The CIR framework extends naturally to more sophisticated models 2:

    • Multi-factor CIR: Multiple independent square-root processes capturing different sources of yield curve movement 4
    • CIR++: Adding a deterministic shift to fit the initial yield curve, analogous to Hull-White 5
    • Jump-extended CIR: Incorporating jumps in the short rate for capturing discrete policy changes
    • Heston model: Using CIR dynamics for the variance in equity option pricing 6
    • Affine jump-diffusion: General framework encompassing CIR as special case 2

    The mathematical elegance and practical properties of the CIR model 1 have made it a cornerstone of modern financial modeling, with applications extending far beyond interest rate modeling to credit risk, equity derivatives, and commodities 2.
