    1. Black-Scholes equation

    Cristian Arley Quintero Gonzalez

    About these lecture notes

    This article adapts my older, dated lecture notes on the Black-Scholes equation. The PDF was generated in October 2023. It remains available as the original reference.

    The complete derivation is retained here, including equations (1)–(19), all four footnotes, and all eight sidenotes. Wording and cross-references have been edited for readability. The lognormal-distribution statement and the interpretation of the rebalanced hedge are clarified below rather than repeating mistakes from the older notes.

    1.1 Why revisit the theory?

    Whether you work as a quant or are preparing to become one, you will sometimes need to revisit methods, definitions, and model assumptions. Quantitative finance spans statistics, programming, finance, and sometimes machine learning. Nobody keeps every detail in memory.

    These notes provide a refresher on the foundations of option valuation, beginning with the Black-Scholes equation.1 Where relevant, implementation examples can be linked from the author's repository as they become available.

    The derivation is useful because the Black-Scholes framework is a starting point for understanding both vanilla and exotic options. It is not a universal model for every contract.

    Sidenote 1: exotic derivatives

    Exotic derivatives differ from standard, or vanilla, contracts through features such as their payoff structure, exercise or expiry arrangements, and strike specification.

    1.2 Assumptions

    The basic model makes the following assumptions:

    • The risky asset follows geometric Brownian motion (GBM), with constant volatility.
    • The underlying pays no dividends.
    • Trading is frictionless, without taxes or transaction fees.
    • The risk-free interest rate is constant.
    • The market admits no arbitrage.

    These are modeling assumptions, not descriptions of every real market. Constant volatility, for example, is a useful simplification but does not reproduce the implied-volatility smile.

    For the continuous hedge used below, we also assume that positions can be rebalanced continuously and financed at the same risk-free rate. The option value must be sufficiently smooth before maturity for Itô's lemma to apply.

    1.3 Geometric Brownian motion

    GBM is widely used in finance because, starting from a positive price, its solution remains positive. Its stochastic differential equation is:2

    \[ \begin{aligned} dS &= \mu S\,dt+\sigma S\,dW. \end{aligned} \tag{1}\label{eq:bs-notes-1} \]

    Here \(W\) is a Wiener process, \(\mu\) is the drift, and \(\sigma\) is constant volatility. With \(W_0=0\), the solution is:

    \[ \begin{aligned} S_t &= S_0\exp\left[\left(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t+\sigma W_t\right]. \end{aligned} \tag{2}\label{eq:bs-notes-2} \]

    Taking logarithms makes the distribution explicit:

    \[ \log S_t\sim\mathcal N\left( \log S_0+\left(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t,\, \sigma^2t \right). \]

    Equivalently,

    \[ S_t\sim\operatorname{Lognormal}\left( \log S_0+\left(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t,\, \sigma^2t \right). \]

    Correction to the older distribution statement

    The PDF calls the price lognormal but writes a normal-distribution symbol for \(S\) and misplaces the time factor in its mean. It is \(\log S_t\) that is normal, with mean \(\log S_0+(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t\). The price \(S_t\) is lognormal.

    1.4 The delta-hedged portfolio

    The standard derivation starts with a portfolio containing one long European option of value \(V\) and a short position of \(\Delta\) units of its underlying stock, priced at \(S\).

    Sidenote 2: the underlying

    The stock in the hedge is the same underlying asset that drives the option's value. Its price follows the GBM specified in equation (1).

    Before solving for the option price, we can describe its inputs as \(V(S,t,\sigma,\mu,K,T,r)\). These include the current spot, time, volatility, drift, strike, maturity, and risk-free rate.

    Sidenote 3: familiar pricing inputs

    These are the inputs discussed in the older notes alongside the Black-Scholes formula. There is an important distinction: the physical drift \(\mu\) describes the stock process, but it will disappear from the no-arbitrage pricing equation.

    The parameters \(\mu,K,r,T,\sigma\) are treated as fixed in this derivation. We therefore write the unknown option value simply as \(V(S,t)\), leaving its fixed inputs implicit.

    Sidenote 4: constant volatility

    Treating volatility as constant is one of the model's main limitations. The simplification makes the argument tractable but does not imply that observed volatility is constant.

    The marked value of the option-and-stock position is:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \Pi &= V(S,t)-\Delta S. \end{aligned} \tag{3}\label{eq:bs-notes-3} \]

    The minus sign represents a short stock position when \(\Delta>0\). Holding the chosen hedge fixed over the next infinitesimal interval, the position's gain is:

    \[ \begin{aligned} d\Pi &= dV-\Delta\,dS. \end{aligned} \tag{4}\label{eq:bs-notes-4} \]

    A local hedge is not a free rebalancing rule

    Equation (4) describes gains while the current stock holding is fixed. If \(\Delta\) changes, differentiating \(V-\Delta S\) as a time-varying marked value also produces rebalancing terms. Those trades must be funded.

    A precise self-financing replication writes \(V=\Delta S+B\), where \(B\) is the value held in the cash account, and requires \(dV=\Delta\,dS+rB\,dt\). Consequently, the locally hedged gain is \(dV-\Delta\,dS=r(V-\Delta S)\,dt\). This justifies the no-arbitrage step below without assuming that changes in the stock hedge cost nothing.

    1.4.1 Analyze the variation in the option value

    To understand \(dV\), first expand \(V\) in its two changing inputs, \(S\) and \(t\). The second-order terms used in the notes are:

    \[ \begin{aligned} dV={}&\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dS +\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,dt +\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}(dS)^2\\ &+\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial t^2}(dt)^2 +\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S\partial t}\,dS\,dt. \end{aligned} \tag{5}\label{eq:bs-notes-5} \]

    Sidenote 5: Itô's lemma

    Itô's lemma gives the differential of a time-dependent function of a stochastic process. The Taylor expansion is a way to motivate it. The rigorous stochastic limit retains the quadratic-variation term that ordinary first-order calculus would miss.

    In Itô calculus, \((dt)^2=0\) and \(dS\,dt=0\) in the differential multiplication rules. We therefore retain:

    \[ \begin{aligned} dV={}&\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dS +\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,dt +\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}(dS)^2. \end{aligned} \tag{6}\label{eq:bs-notes-6} \]

    Sidenote 6: why some terms disappear

    Over an interval of length \(dt\), Brownian increments have size of order \(\sqrt{dt}\). Their squares contribute at order \(dt\), while \((dt)^2\) and products of \(dt\) with Brownian increments vanish in the limit. The key is the different scaling of stochastic and ordinary increments, not merely that every increment is small.

    1.4.2 Incorporate the squared stock increment

    Substitute the GBM increment into \((dS)^2\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} (dS)^2&=(\mu S\,dt+\sigma S\,dW)^2. \end{aligned} \tag{7}\label{eq:bs-notes-7} \]

    Expanding gives:

    \[ \begin{aligned} (dS)^2 &=\mu^2S^2(dt)^2+\sigma^2S^2(dW)^2 +2\mu\sigma S^2\,dt\,dW\\ &=\sigma^2S^2(dW)^2. \end{aligned} \tag{8}\label{eq:bs-notes-8} \]

    Using \((dW)^2=dt\) in the Itô sense and substituting into equation (6):

    \[ \begin{aligned} dV &=\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dS +\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,dt +\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2S^2\,dt. \end{aligned} \tag{9}\label{eq:bs-notes-9} \]

    Sidenote 7: quadratic variation

    The identity \((dW)^2=dt\) summarizes Brownian quadratic variation, \([W]_t=t\). It does not assert equality of individual random squared increments and elapsed time. Brownian motion can be obtained as a limit of suitably rescaled random walks, written \(W^{(n)}(t)\) in the older notes.

    The PDF points to Shreve's Stochastic Calculus for Finance II: Continuous-Time Models (2004), page 93, a discussion of the Wiener-process differential rule, and Nicolas Privault's lectures.

    1.4.3 Incorporate the option increment into the portfolio gain

    Substitute equation (9) into equation (4):

    \[ \begin{aligned} d\Pi &=\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dS +\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,dt +\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2S^2\,dt -\Delta\,dS. \end{aligned} \tag{10}\label{eq:bs-notes-10} \]

    1.4.4 Delta hedging

    The first and last terms in equation (10) both multiply the same stock increment. We can cancel that exposure by choosing:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dS&=\Delta\,dS, \qquad \Delta=\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}. \end{aligned} \tag{11}\label{eq:bs-notes-11} \]

    This is delta hedging. The hedge uses the option's price sensitivity to determine how many units of stock to short. For example, if a call has delta \(0.6\), one unit of that option is locally hedged by shorting \(0.6\) units of the underlying.

    Delta is one of the Greeks, the sensitivities used to describe how an option responds to changing inputs. In this idealized one-factor diffusion model, continuous rebalancing removes the local Brownian exposure. A hedge adjusted only at discrete times can still incur hedging error.

    1.5 The no-arbitrage assumption

    After delta hedging, the remaining local gain has no Brownian component. In a frictionless, arbitrage-free market, it must earn the risk-free rate on the value of the hedged position. Otherwise, an appropriate offsetting cash position would allow a locally riskless profit.3

    Sidenote 8: what no arbitrage means

    The older notes refer to Hirsa and Neftci (2014) for the idea that prices must exclude arbitrage opportunities. In practical terms, arbitrage is a self-financing strategy with no initial net investment, no possibility of loss, and a positive probability of profit.

    Comparing the hedged position with the risk-free account provides the pricing restriction. A riskless investment earning the ordinary risk-free return is not itself an arbitrage.

    Thus the local hedged gain must satisfy \(d\Pi=r\Pi\,dt\). Using the expanded expression:

    \[ \begin{aligned} d\Pi=r\Pi\,dt &=\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\,dS +\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,dt +\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2S^2\,dt -\Delta\,dS. \end{aligned} \tag{12}\label{eq:bs-notes-12} \]

    Substitute \(\Pi=V-\Delta S\) and cancel the stock-increment terms using equation (11):

    \[ \begin{aligned} r(V-\Delta S)\,dt &=\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,dt +\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2S^2\,dt. \end{aligned} \tag{13}\label{eq:bs-notes-13} \]

    Factor out the common time increment:

    \[ \begin{aligned} (rV-r\Delta S)\,dt &=\left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} +\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2S^2 \right)dt. \end{aligned} \tag{14}\label{eq:bs-notes-14} \]

    Equating the drift coefficients gives:

    \[ \begin{aligned} rV-r\Delta S &=\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} +\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2S^2. \end{aligned} \tag{15}\label{eq:bs-notes-15} \]

    1.6 The Black-Scholes equation

    Move all terms in equation (15) to one side:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} +\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2S^2 +r\Delta S-rV&=0. \end{aligned} \tag{16}\label{eq:bs-notes-16} \]

    Finally, substitute \(\Delta=\partial V/\partial S\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial V}{\partial t} +\frac12\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2S^2 +r\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}S-rV&=0. \end{aligned} \tag{17}\label{eq:bs-notes-17} \]

    This is the Black-Scholes partial differential equation for a non-dividend-paying underlying under the stated assumptions. Notice that \(\mu\) has disappeared. The argument prices a replicable claim through hedging and no arbitrage rather than through an investor's estimate of the stock's expected return.

    The equation alone does not specify a unique option price. It must be paired with the contract's payoff and suitable boundary or growth conditions. For example, a European call has terminal payoff \(V(S,T)=\max(S-K,0)\), while a European put has \(V(S,T)=\max(K-S,0)\). These payoff examples are explanatory additions to the older derivation.

    1.6.1 The equation in Greeks

    Greeks measure how the derivative's price responds when an input changes. The three needed here are:4

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Greeks}= \begin{cases} \Gamma=\dfrac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2},\\ \Delta=\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial S},\\ \Theta=\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial t}. \end{cases} \end{aligned} \tag{18}\label{eq:bs-notes-18} \]

    Substituting into equation (17):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \Theta+\frac12\Gamma\sigma^2S^2+\Delta rS-rV&=0. \end{aligned} \tag{19}\label{eq:bs-notes-19} \]

    Here \(\Theta\) is the derivative with respect to calendar time \(t\). If time to maturity \(\tau=T-t\) is used instead, then \(\partial V/\partial t=-\partial V/\partial\tau\). Keeping this convention explicit avoids changing the sign of the time term accidentally.

    In words, the equation balances the passage of time, curvature with respect to the stock price, the stock hedge's financing, and the option's financing. Together they leave no arbitrage under the model.
