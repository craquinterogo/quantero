    1. Black-Scholes and the Flat Volatility Surface

    The Black-Scholes model 12 is the foundational constant-volatility framework and the natural starting point for understanding the volatility surface. Every more complex model in this section can be interpreted as a correction or extension of Black-Scholes, and the vocabulary of the field — implied volatility, the smile, local volatility — is defined relative to what Black-Scholes predicts. That prediction is simple and radical: the implied volatility surface is a flat plane. One number, \(\sigma\), prices every option regardless of its strike or maturity.

    1.1 From Hedging to a PDE

    Fischer Black and Myron Scholes derived their formula through a purely arbitrage-based argument. Consider a European call option with value \(C(S, t)\) on an underlying asset price \(S\). The asset follows a geometric Brownian motion:

    \[ \begin{equation} dS(t) = \mu S(t)\,dt + \sigma S(t)\,dW(t), \label{eq:bs_gbm} \end{equation} \]

    where \(\mu\) is the drift, \(\sigma > 0\) is the constant volatility, and \(W(t)\) is a standard Brownian motion under the physical measure. By Itô's lemma the option value changes as:

    \[ \begin{equation} dC = \left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \mu S \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\right)dt + \sigma S \frac{\partial C}{\partial S}\,dW(t). \label{eq:bs_ito} \end{equation} \]

    Now form a self-financing portfolio \(\Pi = C - \Delta S\) where \(\Delta = \partial C / \partial S\) (the delta hedge). The stochastic increments cancel exactly:

    \[ d\Pi = dC - \Delta\,dS = \left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\right)dt. \]

    This portfolio is instantaneously riskless — it contains no \(dW\) term. In the absence of arbitrage it must earn the risk-free rate \(r\):

    \[ d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt = r(C - \Delta S)\,dt. \]

    Setting the two expressions for \(d\Pi\) equal and substituting \(\Delta = \partial C / \partial S\) yields the Black-Scholes PDE:

    \[ \begin{equation} \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} - rC = 0. \label{eq:bs_pde} \end{equation} \]

    This is a parabolic PDE in \((S, t)\) with terminal condition \(C(S, T) = \max(S - K, 0)\) for a European call.

    1.2 Solving the PDE: The Black-Scholes Formula

    The PDE \eqref{eq:bs_pde} can be transformed into the heat equation by the substitution \(S = Ke^x\), \(t = T - \tau/(\sigma^2/2)\), after which the standard Fourier solution over Gaussian kernels applies. The result is the celebrated closed-form formula. Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) and allowing for a continuous dividend yield \(q\):

    \[ \begin{equation} C(S, K, \tau, r, q, \sigma) = S e^{-q\tau}\Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_2), \label{eq:bs_call} \end{equation} \]
    \[ \begin{equation} P(S, K, \tau, r, q, \sigma) = K e^{-r\tau}\Phi(-d_2) - S e^{-q\tau}\Phi(-d_1), \label{eq:bs_put} \end{equation} \]

    where

    \[ \begin{equation} d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r - q + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}. \label{eq:bs_d1d2} \end{equation} \]

    The two terms in \eqref{eq:bs_call} have a natural interpretation. \(Se^{-q\tau}\Phi(d_1)\) is the present value of the asset conditional on exercise, and \(Ke^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_2)\) is the present value of the strike payment times the risk-neutral probability of exercise. The formula is fully determined by observables \((S, K, \tau, r, q)\) and the single free parameter \(\sigma\).

    1.3 Implied Volatility as the Score of Black-Scholes

    The Black-Scholes formula \eqref{eq:bs_call} is monotonically increasing in \(\sigma\) for any \((S, K, \tau, r, q)\) with \(K, \tau > 0\). Given a market price \(C^{\text{mkt}}\) for an option, the implied volatility \(\hat\sigma\) is the unique solution to

    \[ \begin{equation} C(S, K, \tau, r, q, \hat\sigma) = C^{\text{mkt}}. \label{eq:imp_vol} \end{equation} \]

    Implied volatility is nothing more than the model parameter that makes Black-Scholes reproduce the observed price. It is not an observable itself — it is a transformation of price under the assumption that Black-Scholes is correct.

    1.4 The Flat Surface Prediction

    Under Black-Scholes, \(\sigma\) is constant — the same for all \(K\) and all \(T\). If the Black-Scholes world were exactly true, inverting \eqref{eq:imp_vol} for every traded option would return exactly the same value \(\hat\sigma = \sigma\) regardless of strike or maturity. The volatility surface \(\hat\sigma(K, T)\) would be a flat plane.

    This is the defining characteristic of the constant-volatility model and the baseline against which all other models are measured. The moment market prices are collected and \eqref{eq:imp_vol} is inverted, one observes that \(\hat\sigma\) varies across \((K, T)\) in a rich, structured pattern:

    The smile and skew. For a fixed maturity, implied volatility is typically higher for deep out-of-the-money puts than for at-the-money options in equity markets (a negative skew), and may be elevated symmetrically in FX markets (a smile). Black-Scholes predicts neither: it produces a flat line.

    The term structure. For a fixed strike, implied volatility is not constant in maturity. Short-dated options embed more uncertainty per unit time than long-dated ones during stressed periods. Black-Scholes is silent on this phenomenon.

    The volatility smile (post-1987). The equity skew is a post-crash phenomenon. Before October 1987 the implied volatility surface was nearly flat — consistent with Black-Scholes. The crash of October 19 taught the market that large downward jumps are more likely than log-normality implies, and the skew has been present ever since. This single empirical fact motivates the entire volatility surface modeling industry.

    1.5 Greeks: Sensitivities Implied by Constant Volatility

    The partial derivatives of \eqref{eq:bs_call} are the Black-Scholes Greeks, which remain the benchmark hedging quantities even when more complex models are used.

    \[ \Delta = \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} = e^{-q\tau}\Phi(d_1), \qquad \Gamma = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} = \frac{e^{-q\tau}\phi(d_1)}{S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \]
    \[ \mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma} = S e^{-q\tau}\phi(d_1)\sqrt{\tau}, \qquad \Theta = \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = -\frac{S e^{-q\tau}\phi(d_1)\sigma}{2\sqrt{\tau}} - rKe^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_2) + qSe^{-q\tau}\Phi(d_1), \]

    where \(\phi\) is the standard normal density. Vega \(\mathcal{V}\) is strictly positive, confirming that the Black-Scholes price is monotone in \(\sigma\) and that \eqref{eq:imp_vol} has a unique solution.

    1.6 Limitations Motivating Surface Models

    The Black-Scholes model fails empirically on three counts that are visible directly from the volatility surface.

    First, log-normality is empirically wrong. Equity returns have fat tails and negative skewness; the log-normal assumption systematically underprices out-of-the-money puts and overprices out-of-the-money calls.

    Second, volatility is not constant. Historical volatility varies over time — periods of calm alternate with turbulent regimes. The model treats \(\sigma\) as fixed, which is manifestly incorrect. Any risk manager who monitors realized volatility would immediately reject this assumption.

    Third, perfect hedging is not achievable in continuous time. The derivation above relies on continuous, costless rebalancing of the delta hedge. Transaction costs, discrete trading intervals, and stochastic volatility all introduce residual hedge errors that the flat-surface model ignores.

    The remaining models in this section are organized precisely around which of these limitations they address: local volatility relaxes the constancy of \(\sigma\) as a function of \((S, t)\); stochastic volatility models make \(\sigma\) itself a random process; jump models add discontinuous price movements; hybrid models combine several of these ingredients; rough volatility addresses the incorrect assumption about the regularity of the volatility process itself.
