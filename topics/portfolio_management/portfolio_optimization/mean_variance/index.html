    Mean Variance Model

    Overview

    The Mean-Variance optimization, developed by Harry Markowitz in 1952, is a foundational portfolio theory that helps investors construct efficient portfolios. Nonetheless, this model is widely used though, the result and modeling assumptions have been questioned by the industry over last decades. Let's see what is it about.

    Markowitz starts his article pointing out two stages in porfolio selection:

    • The first one implies there is accessible information and there is an assumption of asset's future performance of available securities.
    • Second stage assesses the future performance and ends up in picking up the portfolio set of assets.

    Main parameters

    He is clear on highlighting his model is focused in the second one. Moreover, he supports his vision of portfolio selection by means geometrical relations between expected returns and variance of returns. These are is the backbone of this model. So, it's worth to mention the variance is the measure of risk in this framework. Not for nothing many people explain the model refering to them as tradeoff between risk-return.

    Besides, there is an apparent duality between expected returns and variance of returns, as you can fix only one of them whereas freeing the another one. This takes you to the fist selection, keeping an discounted value of future returns but freeing the variance thereof, or, holding a level of risk by means a fixed variance then freeing the return possibilities. Consequently, diversification turned a buzzword in finance, but it's worth recalling what Markowitz subtly highlights, which is "... diversification is both observed and sensible;". This clearly makes echo with the perspective of John Burr Williams, in his book named The Theory Of Investment Value. Williams describes a fascinating treat of markets, and it is that ".. Today's opinion will make today's price; tomorrow's opinion, tomorrow's price .. Thus marginal opinion will determine market price ..". 1

    Now, having replicated Markowitz's idea, let's jumpt to the maths. The model construction is upon an analitical solution that assumess in the market there are \(N\) securities, each identified with subindex \(i\).

    Portfolio Returns

    The portfolio's return is expressed as a weighted discounted return of assets that comprise the portfolio. This becomes clear once you read Markowitz's article directly. Many practitioners, are prone to using the non-discounted return.. Because of the latter, in my mind the first image I had was stochastic discount factors, that I'll talk about in another post. Now, let's represent each asset's return as \(r_{i,t}\), emphasizing the sub-index \((i,t)\), which means the return of asset \(i\) expected in future time \(t\). Furtheremore, the discount factor applied to each return is depicted as \(d_{i,t}\), meaning is applied on each asset \(i\) in the instant \(t\). It looks like ...

    \[ \begin{equation} R_p=\sum_{t=1}^\infty \sum_{i=1}^N d_{i,t}r_{i,t}w_i \label{eq:markowitz_portfolio_return_orig} \end{equation} \]

    where \(w_i\) is the relative amount invested in the asset \(i\), that Markowitz named as \(X\) 1. In addition, Markowitz also adds a restriction on the weights by setting them as strictly possitive \(w_i \ge 0\), which means the model doesn't support short positions. Similarlly, the sum of all positions must be equal to 1, which make sense if we consider the relative weight is with respect to the portfolio value, a metric that is distorsioned if short positions are allowed.

    Then, because of sum properties, it's possible to rewrite equation \(\eqref{eq:markowitz_portfolio_return_orig}\) such that the time dependend variables can be replaced as shown in \(\eqref{eq:markowitz_portfolio_return}\), where \(R_i\) corresponds to the discounted future return of asset \(i\).

    \[ \begin{equation} R_p= \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \left(\sum_{t=1}^\infty d_{i,t}r_{i,t}\right) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i R_i \label{eq:markowitz_portfolio_return} \end{equation} \]

    Don't panic!, Markowitz also made the first version of the model even simpler. He changed the variable \(R_i\) as discounted future return of the i-asset by something that he calls "the flow of returns", depicted by \(r_i\) 2. This change makes easier the treatment of optimization as the portfolio return is now \(R_p=\sum w_i r_i\).
