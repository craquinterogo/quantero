    1. Volatility Surface Models

    The implied volatility surface \(\hat\sigma(K, T)\) — the map from strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\) to the Black-Scholes volatility that reproduces each observed option price — is one of the most information-rich objects in financial markets. Any model that prices options must take a position on its shape, dynamics, and calibration, and the long history of that effort has produced a well-structured taxonomy of approaches [^gatheral2006][^andersen2010].

    This section organises volatility surface models into six progressively richer classes. Each class addresses a specific deficiency of the one before it, and understanding why each extension is necessary is as important as knowing the formulas.

    1.1 Class 1 — Constant Volatility

    The Black-Scholes model [^black_scholes_1973] assumes a single constant \(\sigma\) for all strikes and maturities. The implied surface it produces is a flat plane. This is the baseline: every other model can be understood as a correction to it, and the vocabulary of the field is defined relative to how far market prices deviate from this flat prediction. The Black-Scholes and the flat surface article derives the model from first principles, establishes the Black-Scholes PDE via the delta-hedging argument, and provides the closed-form call and put formulas. The canvas there directly contrasts the flat Black-Scholes surface against a market surface exhibiting skew and term structure.

    1.2 Class 2 — Local Volatility

    Local volatility models make the volatility a deterministic function of the current spot price and time: \(\sigma = \sigma(S, t)\). This single change is sufficient to fit any arbitrage-free vanilla surface exactly, while preserving the tractability of a one-factor model. The fundamental result — Dupire's formula — inverts the relationship between option prices and the local volatility function, making calibration a direct computation rather than an optimisation [^dupire1994].

    The limitation is forward-volatility dynamics: while the vanilla surface is reproduced, the model predicts future smile shapes that differ systematically from what stochastic volatility models (and the market) produce. Forward-starting products and cliquets are particularly sensitive to this difference.

    Dupire Local Volatility Model — Derivation of Dupire's PDE and formula, implementation in strike-maturity and log-moneyness coordinates, and numerical calibration.

    1.3 Class 3 — Stochastic Volatility

    Stochastic volatility models introduce a second stochastic factor to govern the evolution of instantaneous volatility \(v(t)\). Volatility becomes unpredictable — it has its own Brownian driver, its own mean-reversion level and speed, and a correlation with the spot process that generates skew. These models capture realistic volatility dynamics and can be calibrated to both the vanilla surface and the dynamics of realised volatility, but they require Fourier pricing methods and numerical calibration procedures [^heston1993][^hagan2002].

    Heston Model — The CIR square-root variance process, the characteristic function via the affine Riccati ODEs, Fourier pricing with the Carr-Madan approach, and the Feller condition.

    SABR Model — The CEV-type forward dynamics with log-normal vol-of-vol, Hagan's asymptotic implied volatility formula, and its use as the market's standard backbone for interest rate derivatives.

    1.4 Class 4 — Jump Models

    Jump models add a Poisson-driven compound jump component to the log-price dynamics. Diffusions cannot reproduce the fat tails and steep short-dated skews observed in equity markets because, under any continuous process, the probability of a large return in a short time vanishes exponentially. Jumps fix this by assigning finite probability to discrete price changes of arbitrary size [^merton_1976][^cont_tankov_2004].

    The Merton model is the foundational member of this class: log-normally distributed jumps superimposed on geometric Brownian motion, with a pricing formula that is an infinite series of Black-Scholes terms weighted by Poisson probabilities. Its main limitation is that the smile it generates decays too quickly with maturity, motivating the hybrid models in Class 5.

    Merton Jump-Diffusion Model — Derivation of the risk-neutral dynamics, conditional log-price distribution, Merton's series pricing formula, and the interactive smile canvas showing the effect of jump intensity, mean size, and jump vol.

    Bates Model — Heston stochastic volatility extended with Merton-style log-normal jumps, characteristic function derivation, and the interplay between jump and diffusive skew.

    1.5 Class 5 — Hybrid Models

    Hybrid models combine two or more of the above ingredients to overcome individual limitations. The central trade-off is between calibration fidelity, pricing tractability, and realistic forward dynamics.

    The Local Stochastic Volatility (LSV) model is the industry standard for equity exotics desks: it combines a stochastic volatility process (typically Heston) with a deterministic leverage function \(L(S, t)\) that rescales instantaneous vol to reproduce the vanilla surface exactly. The leverage function inherits the fitting power of local vol while the stochastic component preserves realistic vol-of-vol dynamics.

    The SVI parametrisation is a different kind of hybrid: rather than specifying model dynamics, it fits a functional form directly to the observed smile that is (a) parsimonious — five parameters per slice — and (b) free of static arbitrage by construction. It is widely used for fast interpolation and extrapolation of the vanilla surface before running more complex models.

    Local Stochastic Volatility (LSV) — Leverage function construction via the Gyöngy lemma, Fokker-Planck calibration, particle methods, and the pricing-hedging duality between local vol and stochastic vol.

    SVI Parametrisation — The five-parameter raw SVI, arbitrage-free conditions (Gatheral-Jacquier), surface SVI for the full \((K, T)\) grid, and calibration by least squares.

    1.6 Class 6 — Rough Volatility

    Rough volatility models address a subtler empirical finding: the Hurst exponent of realised volatility time series is significantly less than \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), indicating that volatility paths are rougher — more irregular — than any standard Brownian motion can produce [^gatheral_2018]. The classical stochastic volatility models all use processes with \(H = \tfrac{1}{2}\) (or higher), which oversmooth volatility relative to what is observed.

    The rough fractional stochastic volatility (rFSV) model and its relatives use the Riemann-Liouville fractional Brownian motion with \(H \approx 0.1\) to drive instantaneous variance. This single change — replacing the Brownian driver of the variance process with a rough fractional process — simultaneously explains the power-law decay of the volatility autocorrelation, the level and slope of the ATM skew, and its term structure scaling \(\sim T^{H-1/2}\).

    Rough Volatility — Empirical evidence for roughness, the fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck driver, rBergomi and rFSV models, short-time skew asymptotics, and Monte Carlo simulation via the hybrid scheme.
