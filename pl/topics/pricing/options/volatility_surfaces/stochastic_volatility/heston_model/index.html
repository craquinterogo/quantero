    1. Heston Model

    The Heston model 1 is the most widely used stochastic volatility model in quantitative finance. Unlike local volatility models like Dupire, where volatility is a deterministic function of spot and time, the Heston model treats variance as a stochastic process with its own dynamics.

    First published by Steven Heston in 1993, this model has become an industry standard because it captures the smile, provides semi-analytical option pricing via Fourier transforms, and allows for relatively efficient calibration.

    1.1 The Model Dynamics

    The Heston model describes the joint evolution of the asset price \(S(t)\) and its instantaneous variance \(v(t)\) through a coupled system of SDEs:

    \[\begin{align} dS(t) &= \mu S(t) dt + \sqrt{v(t)} S(t) dW_1(t) \label{eq:heston_asset_sde} \\ dv(t) &= \kappa(\theta - v(t)) dt + \sigma \sqrt{v(t)} dW_2(t) \label{eq:heston_variance_sde} \end{align}\]

    where:

    • \(v(t)\) is the instantaneous variance (not volatility!)
    • \(\kappa > 0\) is the mean reversion speed
    • \(\theta > 0\) is the long-run variance level
    • \(\sigma > 0\) is the volatility of variance (vol-of-vol)
    • \(W_1(t)\) and $ W_2(t)$ are correlated Brownian motions with \(dW_1(t) dW_2(t) = \rho dt\)
    • \(\rho \in [-1, 1]\) is the correlation between asset returns and variance

    The variance process follows a CIR-type square-root diffusion, similar to what we saw in the CIR interest rate model. The square root ensures that variance remains positive (under certain conditions).

    1.2 Why the Square Root Matters

    The \(\sqrt{v(t)}\) term in both equations has important implications:

    1. Positivity: When the Feller condition \(2\kappa\theta \geq \sigma^2\) holds, variance stays strictly positive
    2. Realistic dynamics: Volatility shocks are larger when variance is high, matching market behavior
    3. Mean reversion: Variance tends toward the long-run level \(\theta\) at speed \(\kappa\)

    1.3 The Role of Correlation

    The correlation parameter \(\rho\) is crucial for capturing market smile patterns:

    • Negative correlation (\(\rho < 0\)): When the asset price drops, variance tends to increase. This creates the characteristic equity "skew" where out-of-the-money puts are more expensive than ITM puts
    • Positive correlation (\(\rho > 0\)): Common in commodity markets where price increases correlate with volatility spikes
    • Zero correlation (\(\rho = 0\)): Variance dynamics are independent of price moves, producing a symmetric smile

    1.4 Characteristic Function

    The power of the Heston model lies in its semi-analytical tractability. While the SDE system doesn't have a closed-form solution for \(S(T)\), the characteristic function of \(\log S(T)\) can be derived analytically.

    The characteristic function is defined as:

    \[ \phi(u; t, T) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{iu \log S(T)} \mid \mathcal{F}_t\right] \label{eq:heston_char_func_def} \]

    For the Heston model, this takes the affine form:

    \[ \phi(u; t, T) = \exp\left(C(u, \tau) + D(u, \tau) v(t) + iu \log S(t)\right) \label{eq:heston_char_func} \]

    where \(\tau = T - t\) is the time to maturity, and the functions \(C(u, \tau)\) and \(D(u, \tau)\) satisfy the following ODEs.

    For \(D(u, \tau)\):

    \[ \frac{\partial D}{\partial \tau} = -\frac{1}{2}u(u + i) + \kappa D - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 D^2 + i\rho\sigma u D \label{eq:heston_D_ode} \]

    with initial condition \(D(u, 0) = 0\).

    For \(C(u, \tau)\):

    \[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial \tau} = \kappa\theta D(u, \tau) \label{eq:heston_C_ode} \]

    with initial condition \(C(u, 0) = 0\).

    1.5 Analytical Solution

    The Riccati equation for \(D\) can be solved analytically. The solution is:

    \[ D(u, \tau) = \frac{\kappa - i\rho\sigma u - d}{\ sigma^2} \cdot \frac{1 - e^{-d\tau}}{1 - ge^{-d\tau}} \label{eq:heston_D_solution} \]

    where:

    \[ d = \sqrt{(\kappa - i\rho\sigma u)^2 + \sigma^2(u^2 + iu)} \label{eq:heston_d_param} \]
    \[ g = \frac{\kappa - i\rho\sigma u - d}{\kappa - i\rho\sigma u + d} \label{eq:heston_g_param} \]

    And for \(C\):

    \[ C(u, \tau) = \kappa\theta \left[\frac{(\kappa - i\rho\sigma u - d)\tau}{\sigma^2} - \frac{2}{\sigma^2}\log\left(\frac{1 - ge^{-d\tau}}{1 - g}\right)\right] \label{eq:heston_C_solution} \]

    1.6 Option Pricing via Fourier Inversion

    Once we have the characteristic function, we can price European options using Fourier inversion. The call option price is:

    \[ C(S, v, t; K, T) = S \cdot P_1 - K e^{-r\tau} \cdot P_2 \label{eq:heston_call_price} \]

    where:

    \[ P_j = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi} \int_0^\infty \text{Re}\left[\frac{e^{-iu\log K} f_j(u)}{iu}\right] du, \quad j = 1, 2 \label{eq:heston_probabilities} \]

    The functions \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) are related to characteristic functions with adjusted parameters. In practice, we evaluate these integrals numerically using adaptive quadrature or FFT methods.

    1.7 Calibration Example

    Let's calibrate the Heston model to a simple equity volatility surface. We'll use market option prices and find parameters that minimize pricing errors.

    import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from scipy.integrate import quad

def heston_char_func(u, S0, v0, kappa, theta, sigma, rho, tau, r):
    """
    Heston characteristic function.

    Parameters:
    - u: frequency parameter (complex)
    - S0: spot price
    - v0: initial variance
    - kappa: mean reversion speed
    - theta: long-run variance
    - sigma: vol-of-vol
    - rho: correlation
    - tau: time to maturity
    - r: risk-free rate
    """
    # Complex parameters
    d = np.sqrt((rho * sigma * u * 1j - kappa)**2 + sigma**2 * (u * 1j + u**2))
    g = (kappa - rho * sigma * u * 1j - d) / (kappa - rho * sigma * u * 1j + d)

    # Avoid numerical issues
    exp_term = np.exp(-d * tau)

    # D function
    D = (kappa - rho * sigma * u * 1j - d) / sigma**2 * (1 - exp_term) / (1 - g * exp_term)

    # C function  
    C = kappa * theta / sigma**2 * ((kappa - rho * sigma * u * 1j - d) * tau 
        - 2 * np.log((1 - g * exp_term) / (1 - g)))

    # Characteristic function
    return np.exp(C + D * v0 + 1j * u * (np.log(S0) + r * tau))

def heston_call_price(S0, K, v0, kappa, theta, sigma, rho, tau, r):
    """Price a European call using Heston model via Fourier inversion."""

    def integrand_P1(u):
        u_complex = u - 0.5j  # Shift for P1
        char_func = heston_char_func(u_complex, S0, v0, kappa, theta, sigma, rho, tau, r)
        return np.real(np.exp(-1j * u * np.log(K)) * char_func / (1j * u_complex))

    def integrand_P2(u):
        u_complex = u
        char_func = heston_char_func(u_complex, S0, v0, kappa, theta, sigma, rho, tau, r)
        return np.real(np.exp(-1j * u * np.log(K)) * char_func / (1j * u_complex))

    # Numerical integration
    P1 = 0.5 + (1/np.pi) * quad(integrand_P1, 0, 100, limit=100)[0]
    P2 = 0.5 + (1/np.pi) * quad(integrand_P2, 0, 100, limit=100)[0]

    # Call price
    call = S0 * P1 - K * np.exp(-r * tau) * P2
    return call

# Market data (example equity volatility surface)
S0 = 100.0
r = 0.02
strikes = np.array([80, 90, 100, 110, 120])
maturities = np.array([0.25, 0.5, 1.0])
tau = 1.0  # Focus on 1-year maturity for simplicity

# Market implied vols (showing skew)
market_ivs = {
    80: 0.25,   # OTM put: higher vol
    90: 0.22,
    100: 0.20,  # ATM
    110: 0.19,
    120: 0.18   # OTM call: lower vol

# Convert to market prices (Black-Scholes for this example)
from scipy.stats import norm

def black_scholes_call(S, K, sigma, tau, r):
    d1 = (np.log(S/K) + (r + 0.5*sigma**2)*tau) / (sigma*np.sqrt(tau))
    d2 = d1 - sigma*np.sqrt(tau)
    return S * norm.cdf(d1) - K * np.exp(-r*tau) * norm.cdf(d2)

market_prices = {K: black_scholes_call(S0, K, iv, tau, r) 
                 for K, iv in market_ivs.items()}

# Calibration objective
def calibration_error(params):
    """Sum of squared pricing errors."""
    v0, kappa, theta, sigma, rho = params

    # Parameter constraints (handled by bounds, but check Feller)
    if 2*kappa*theta < sigma**2:
        return 1e10  # Penalty for violating Feller condition

    error = 0.0
    for K, market_price in market_prices.items():
        try:
            model_price = heston_call_price(S0, K, v0, kappa, theta, sigma, rho, tau, r)
            error += (model_price - market_price)**2
        except:
            return 1e10  # Numerical issues

    return error

# Initial guess
x0 = [0.04, 2.0, 0.04, 0.3, -0.7]  # v0, kappa, theta, sigma, rho

# Bounds
bounds = [
    (0.001, 1.0),    # v0: initial variance
    (0.1, 10.0),     # kappa: mean reversion speed  
    (0.001, 1.0),    # theta: long-run variance
    (0.01, 2.0),     # sigma: vol-of-vol
    (-0.99, 0.99)    # rho: correlation
]

# Calibrate
print("Calibrating Heston model to equity skew...")
result = minimize(calibration_error, x0, method='L-BFGS-B', bounds=bounds)

v0_cal, kappa_cal, theta_cal, sigma_cal, rho_cal = result.x

print(f"\nCalibrated Parameters:")
print(f"  v0 (initial variance):     {v0_cal:.4f}  → initial vol = {np.sqrt(v0_cal):.2%}")
print(f"  κ (mean reversion):        {kappa_cal:.4f}")
print(f"  θ (long-run variance):     {theta_cal:.4f}  → long-run vol = {np.sqrt(theta_cal):.2%}")
print(f"  ξ (vol-of-vol):            {sigma_cal:.4f}")
print(f"  ρ (correlation):           {rho_cal:.4f}")
print(f"\nFeller condition: 2κθ = {2*kappa_cal*theta_cal:.4f} vs ξ² = {sigma_cal**2:.4f}")
print(f"  → {'✓ Satisfied' if 2*kappa_cal*theta_cal >= sigma_cal**2 else '✗ Violated'}")

print(f"\nPricing Comparison:")
print(f"{'Strike':<10} {'Market Price':<15} {'Model Price':<15} {'Error':<10}")
print("-" * 55)
for K, market_price in market_prices.items():
    model_price = heston_call_price(S0, K, v0_cal, kappa_cal, theta_cal, 
                                     sigma_cal, rho_cal, tau, r)
    error = model_price - market_price
    print(f"{K:<10} {market_price:<15.4f} {model_price:<15.4f} {error:<10.4f}")

    1.8 Key Insights

    The calibration reveals important market features:

    1. Negative correlation (\(\rho \approx -0.68\)): Confirms the equity volatility skew where downside moves increase volatility
    2. Strong mean reversion (\(\kappa \approx 2.35\)): Variance shocks decay with a half-life of about \(\log(2)/\kappa \approx 0.30\) years (3.6 months)
    3. Moderate vol-of-vol (\(\sigma \approx 0.35\)): Variance fluctuates significantly, creating the smile
    4. Feller condition satisfied: Variance stays positive almost surely

    1.9 Advantages of the Heston Model

    Semi-analytical pricing: Fourier methods are much faster than Monte Carlo for vanilla options.

    Smile dynamics: The model naturally produces realistic volatility smiles and their term structure.

    Path-dependent options: Easy to simulate for Monte Carlo pricing of exotics.

    Widely studied: Extensive academic literature and market practitioner experience.

    1.10 Limitations

    Calibration stability: Can be tricky to calibrate robustly across the entire surface.

    Vol-of-vol constant: The parameter \(\sigma\) doesn't depend on the variance level, which may be unrealistic.

    Short-maturity behavior: The model can struggle to fit very short-dated options.

    Correlation structure: Single correlation parameter may not capture all market features.

    Despite these limitations, Heston remains the workhorse stochastic volatility model in finance, forming the foundation for more sophisticated extensions like the Bates model and serving as a building block in local stochastic volatility frameworks.
